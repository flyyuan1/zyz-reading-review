The origin and development of applause

掌声的起源与发展

Whether you clap, cheer or whistle a great public performance, you are following a long tradition.

无论你为一场精彩的公开演出鼓掌、欢呼还是吹口哨，你都在沿袭一种历史悠久的传统。

AScholars aren’t quite sure about the origins of applause, but the form that is still most common, clapping the hands together, was formalized in Western culture during and after performances. During the time of the Roman Republic (509 BC – 27 BC) the common way to end a play was for the chief actor to yell ‘Valete et plaudite!’ (‘Farewell, and applaud!’) thus signaling to the audience that it was time for them to give praise by striking hands and making explosive noise. It’s no surprise, then, that this became a means of crowd manipulation. Rome saw the rise of a professional class of applauders, or ‘people who clapped for their dinner’, hired to infiltrate crowds and manipulate their reaction to performances. Actors would hire a dozen or so people to spread out among their audiences and prolong the applause, or, if they were feeling especially bold, to start ‘spontaneous’ chants and shouts of praise.

学者们并不十分确定掌声的起源。不过，在西方文化中，如今仍最常见的形式——双手相拍——已成为演出期间及结束后的一种固定惯例。罗马共和国时期（公元前509年—公元前27年），一出戏通常由主演高喊“Valete et plaudite!”（“再会，请鼓掌！”）来结束，以此向观众示意：该双手相击、发出响亮的拍手声，表达赞赏了。因此，这种做法后来成为操纵人群的一种手段，也就不足为奇了。在罗马，职业喝彩者这一群体随之兴起；这些人也被称为“靠拍手换饭吃的人”。他们受雇混入观众之中，操纵人们对演出的反应。演员会雇用十二人左右，让他们分散在观众席中，把掌声延续下去；如果演员们格外大胆，还会让这些人发起“自发的”齐声呼喊和赞美声。

BThe Roman Republic came to an end and was replaced by the Roman Empire (27 BC – 476 AD) where applause became a way for leaders to interact directly with their citizens. One of the chief methods they used to estimate their standing with the people was gauging the greetings they got when they entered the arena. Leaders became astute at reading the volume, the rhythm, and the length of the crowd’s claps for clues about how well they were doing as officials and lawmakers. ‘We should think of it as an ancient type of opinion poll,’ says Greg Aldrete, a professor of history. ‘This is how you gauge the people.’ Roman leaders were gathering data about people by listening to applause.

罗马共和国走向终结，取而代之的是罗马帝国（公元前27年—公元476年）；在帝国时期，掌声成了统治者与公民直接互动的一种方式。要判断自己在民众中的地位，统治者采用的一项主要方法，就是评估他们进入竞技场时受到的欢迎程度。他们很善于从人群拍手的音量、节奏和持续时间中寻找线索，判断自己作为官员和立法者表现如何。“我们应当把它看作一种古代的民意调查，”历史学教授 Greg Aldrete 说，“这就是你了解民众的方式。”罗马统治者通过聆听掌声来收集有关民众的数据。

CClever leaders of the ancient world researched their rivals, too. Cicero, the Roman lawyer, would send friends to stand around outside the arena, taking notes to see what kind of greeting each official got when he entered, the better to see who was beloved by the people, and who was not. They had a lot of information to assess. ‘Ancient crowds were more interactive than they are today,’ Aldrete points out. ‘There was a lot of back and forth between speakers and crowds. And particularly in the Greco-Roman world, crowds, especially in cities, were really good at communicating messages through rhythmic clapping, sometimes coupled with shouts.’ The coding was, he says, invaluable when assessing an orator’s fortunes.

古代世界中精明的统治者也会调查自己的对手。罗马律师 Cicero 会派朋友守候在竞技场外，做记录，看看每位官员入场时受到怎样的欢迎，从而更好地判断谁受民众爱戴、谁不受爱戴。他们有大量信息需要评估。“古代人群的互动性比今天的人群更强，”Aldrete 指出，“演讲者与人群之间会频繁地相互回应。尤其是在希腊—罗马世界，人群——特别是城市中的人群——非常善于通过有节奏的拍手来传递信息，有时还会配合呼喊。”他说，这种用掌声节奏传递信息的方式，对于判断一位演说家的前景极有价值。

DAround the first century AD, those systems of applause became more and more elaborate. Applause no longer meant, simply, ‘claps’. While Greco-Roman audiences certainly smacked their palms together the same way we do today, their overall strategies of applause were much more varied than clapping alone. Crowds expressed degrees of approval of the person or persons before them, ranging from claps, to snaps (of the finger and thumb), to waves (of the edge of the toga). The emperor Aurelian decided to replace this gesture by waving a special handkerchief instead, a prop which he then distributed to all Roman citizens, so they would never be without a way to praise him.

大约到了公元1世纪，这些喝彩方式变得越来越复杂。喝彩不再仅仅意味着“拍手”。虽然希腊—罗马观众的确会像今天的我们一样双掌相击，但他们整套表达赞赏的方式远比单纯拍手丰富多样。人群会以一系列不同动作，表达对面前一人或多人程度不等的赞许：从拍手，到用拇指和另一根手指打响指，再到挥动托加袍（toga）的衣边。Aurelian 皇帝决定不再挥动托加袍衣边，改为挥动一块特制手帕；随后，他又把这种手帕分发给所有罗马公民，让他们随时都有办法赞颂他。

EAlthough the style of applause may have changed over the centuries, the need to manipulate remained. The 16th-century French poet Jean Daurat is credited with bringing back the Roman practice of hiring professional applauders. In France, this group became known as a ‘claque’. Daurat handed out tickets to his own performances to people who promised to applaud at the end. By the early 1820s, claques had become institutionalized, with an agency in Paris specializing in the distribution of the claqueurs’ services. The historian William B. Cohen describes the intricate price lists agencies of this kind would hand out to potential patrons: polite clapping would cost this many francs, enthusiastic applause would cost this many. The claque also underwent a kind of classification: the rieurs (laughers) would laugh loudly at the jokes; the pleureurs (criers) would feign tears in reaction to performances; the commissaires (officers) would learn a play or a piece of music by heart and then call attention to its best parts. This practice spread to Milan, to Vienna, and to London before falling out of fashion. The claque lost its power once people became aware of its tricks.

尽管几个世纪以来，喝彩的方式或许发生了变化，但操纵人群的需求依然存在。据认为，16世纪法国诗人 Jean Daurat 重新采用了罗马人雇用职业喝彩者的做法。在法国，这个群体被称为“职业捧场团”（claque）。Daurat 把自己演出的门票发给那些承诺会在演出结束时鼓掌的人。到了19世纪20年代初，职业捧场团已经制度化，巴黎有一家机构专门安排职业捧场者（claqueurs）的服务。历史学家 William B. Cohen 描述了此类机构会发给潜在顾客的繁复价目表：礼貌鼓掌收费若干法郎，热烈喝彩又收费若干法郎。捧场团内部也有分工：rieurs（负责笑的人）会在讲笑话时放声大笑；pleureurs（负责哭的人）会假装因演出而落泪；commissaires（专员）会把一出戏或一段音乐熟记于心，然后引导人们注意其中最精彩的部分。这种做法传到米兰（Milan）、维也纳（Vienna）和伦敦，之后逐渐不再流行。人们一旦识破职业捧场团的伎俩，它便失去了影响力。

FAnd clapping itself evolved. In the 18th century, symphonies and concerts became more serious, aligning themselves with the reverence and spirituality associated with religious ceremonies. Knowing when to maintain silence, as well as when to clap, became a mark of sophistication, a kind of code for audiences to learn. Applause became a matter of ‘do’ or ‘don’t’, so losing many old shades of meaning. Those changes changed performers, too. Applause became much less of a true dialogue with an audience, especially so with opera, where the singers saw it as part of a basic transaction with the crowd. So by the time the 19th century arrived, applause had become standardized and institutionalized, and had become an expectation rather than the reward it used to be.

拍手这种行为本身也在演变。18世纪，交响乐演出和音乐会变得更加严肃，并逐渐带上了宗教仪式中的敬畏感和庄重的精神氛围。懂得何时保持安静、何时鼓掌，成了有修养的标志；这也成了观众需要学习的一套规范。喝彩变成了“鼓掌”或“不鼓掌”的二选一，因此失去了过去许多细微含义。这些变化也改变了表演者。掌声在很大程度上不再构成与观众之间真正的对话；在歌剧中尤其如此，歌唱者把掌声视为自己与观众之间基本交换的一部分。因此，到了19世纪，掌声已经标准化并制度化，成了人们意料之中的事，而不再像过去那样是一种奖赏。

GIn the 21st century we’re reinventing applause, to make it what it used to be: a coded form of communication. More and more the digital world allows us to link and like and share through our devices. By connecting with others in this way, we become part of the performance by participating in it, demonstrating our appreciation by amplifying and extending the show through commenting and praising. Our kind of applause matters more now, in many ways, because it is no longer momentary and short-lived, but is far more permanent instead.

在21世纪，我们正在重新塑造喝彩，使它恢复过去的样子：一种用特定信号传递信息的交流方式。在数字世界中，我们越来越能够借助设备相互连接、点赞和分享。以这种方式与他人建立联系，我们通过参与而成为表演的一部分；我们还借助评论和赞美来扩大演出的影响、延续演出的生命，以此表达欣赏。从许多方面看，我们这种喝彩如今变得更为重要，因为它不再转瞬即逝，而是持久得多。
