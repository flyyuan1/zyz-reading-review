The Myth of the Eight-hour Sleep

八小时睡眠的神话

AWe often worry about lying awake in the middle of the night—but it could be good for you. Indications from both science and history suggest that the eight-hour sleep may not be a natural or inborn pattern for humans. In the early 1990s, psychiatrist Thomas Wehr conducted research which implies just that. Wehr kept a group of people in a darkened room for 14 hours a day for a month. It took some time for their sleep to regulate, but by the fourth week the subjects had settled into a very distinct sleeping pattern. First, they slept for four hours, then woke for one or two hours before falling into a second four-hour sleep. Though sleep scientists were impressed by the implications of the study, the idea that we must sleep for eight consecutive hours persists amongst the general public.

我们常常担心自己半夜醒着，但这或许对你有好处。科学和历史中的迹象都表明，八小时睡眠可能并非人类自然或与生俱来的模式。20世纪90年代初，精神科医生 Thomas Wehr 开展了一项研究，其结果支持这种可能性。Wehr 让一组人连续一个月每天在黑暗房间里待14小时。他们的睡眠过了一段时间才变得规律，但到第4周，受试者已形成一种非常鲜明的睡眠模式：先睡4小时，再清醒1至2小时，之后又睡4小时。睡眠科学家虽然对这项研究的意义印象深刻，公众却仍普遍坚持认为，我们必须连续睡满8小时。

BIn 2001, historian Roger Ekirch of Virginia Tech University in the US published a paper drawn from 16 years of research, revealing historical evidence that humans used to sleep in two distinct periods of time each night. Ekirch’s research found more than 500 references to a segmented sleeping pattern—in diaries, court records, medical books and literature, from the ancient Greeks to tribes in Nigeria. Much like Wehr’s findings, these references describe a first sleep which began not long following sunset. Then there was a waking period of one or two hours and after that a second sleep. During the waking period, people could be quite active. They sometimes got up and moved around the house, although most people stayed in bed, and perhaps read or wrote if they had enough money for candles. In many historic accounts, Ekirch found that people used the time that they were awake between periods of sleep to think about and attempt to analyse their dreams.

2001年，美国弗吉尼亚理工大学（Virginia Tech University）的历史学家 Roger Ekirch 发表了一篇以16年研究为基础的论文，揭示了人类过去每晚分两个不同时段睡眠的历史证据。Ekirch 从日记、法庭记录、医学书籍和文学作品中发现500多处有关分段睡眠模式的记载，所涉及的人群从古希腊人到尼日利亚（Nigeria）的部落。与 Wehr 的发现非常相似，这些记载描述，第一段睡眠在日落后不久开始，随后有1至2小时的清醒期，之后才是第二段睡眠。清醒期间，人们可能相当活跃。有时他们会起床在屋内走动，不过大多数人仍躺在床上；如果有钱购买蜡烛，或许还会阅读或写作。Ekirch 在许多历史记录中发现，人们会利用两段睡眠之间醒着的时间，思考并尝试分析自己的梦。

In his book, Evening’s Empire, historian Craig Koslofsky suggests an explanation for this divided sleep pattern in Europe. ‘Associations with night before the 17th century were not good,’ he writes. He goes on to explain that the streets of the cities and towns at night were often populated by thieves or worse. The streets at night consequently scared many people. Even the wealthy, who could afford to light their way, had better things to spend their nights on.

历史学家 Craig Koslofsky 在《夜晚的帝国》（Evening’s Empire）一书中，为欧洲这种分段睡眠模式提出了一种解释。他写道：“17世纪以前，人们对夜晚的联想并不好。”他接着解释，当时城镇的街道夜间常有小偷，甚至更危险的人出没。因此，夜晚的街道令许多人害怕。即使富人有钱照亮道路，也有更好的方式度过夜晚。

CEkirch found that references to the first and second sleep started to disappear during the late 17th century. The pattern began to alter first among the upper classes in northern Europe and, over the course of the next 200 years, began changing amongst the rest of Western society. By the 1920s, the idea of a first and second sleep had receded entirely from our social consciousness. Ekirch attributes the initial shift to improvements in street and home lighting.

Ekirch 发现，有关第一段和第二段睡眠的记载从17世纪末开始消失。睡眠模式最先在北欧上层阶级中发生变化，并在接下来的200年里逐渐影响西方社会其他阶层。到20世纪20年代，第一段和第二段睡眠的观念已从社会意识中彻底消退。Ekirch 把最初的转变归因于街道和家庭照明的改善。

As the night became a time for all kinds of activity, the length of time people slept declined. In 1667, Paris became the first city in the world to light its streets, using wax candles in glass lamps. It was followed by another French city, Lille, in the same year and by Amsterdam in Holland two years later, where a much more efficient oil-powered lamp was developed. London didn’t light its streets until 1684, but by the end of the 17th century, more than 50 of Europe’s major towns and cities were lit at night. Coffee houses emerged as a fashionable phenomenon and many were open virtually around the clock. Going out at night became commonplace, and spending hours lying in bed was considered a waste of time. ‘People were becoming increasingly time-conscious and sensitive to efficiency, certainly before the 19th century,’ says Ekirch. ‘But the Industrial Revolution intensified that attitude considerably.’ Strong evidence of this shifting attitude is contained in a medical journal from 1829 which urged parents to force their children out of the pattern of first and second sleep.

随着夜晚成为开展各种活动的时间，人们的睡眠时长减少了。1667年，巴黎使用玻璃灯罩内的蜡烛照亮街道，成为世界上第一个这样做的城市。同年，法国另一座城市里尔（Lille）也开始实行街道照明；两年后，荷兰阿姆斯特丹（Amsterdam）也开始这样做，当地还研制出效率高得多的油灯。伦敦直到1684年才照亮街道，但到17世纪末，欧洲已有50多座主要城镇和城市在夜间亮起灯光。咖啡馆成为一种时髦现象，许多咖啡馆几乎全天候营业。夜间外出变得司空见惯，躺在床上耗费数小时则被视为浪费时间。Ekirch 说：“可以肯定，早在19世纪以前，人们就越来越重视时间、关注效率。但工业革命大大强化了这种态度。”1829年的一份医学期刊有力证明了这种态度转变；该刊敦促父母强迫孩子摆脱第一段和第二段睡眠的模式。

DToday, most people seem to have adapted quite well to the eight-hour sleep, but Ekirch believes many sleeping problems may have roots in the human body’s natural preference for divided sleep, as well as in difficulties caused by extended exposure to artificial light in the modern world. This could be the cause of a condition called sleep maintenance insomnia, where people wake during the night and have trouble getting back to sleep. The condition first appears in literature at the end of the 19th century, at the same time as accounts of interrupted sleep disappear.

如今，大多数人似乎已经很好地适应了八小时睡眠。但 Ekirch 认为，许多睡眠问题可能既源于人体天生偏好分段睡眠，也源于现代社会长时间暴露在人造光下造成的困难。这可能引发一种名为睡眠维持性失眠（sleep maintenance insomnia）的病症，患者会在夜间醒来，并且难以再次入睡。19世纪末，这种病症首次出现在文献中，恰好也是分段睡眠记载消失的时期。

ESleep psychologist Gregg Jacobs says that the idea that we must sleep for an extended period of time could be damaging if it makes people who wake up in the night anxious, as this anxiety can itself discourage sleep and is likely to affect waking life too. Jacobs suggests that the waking period between sleeps, when people simply rested and relaxed, could have played an important part in the human capacity to regulate stress naturally. Russell Foster, a professor of circadian (body clock) neuroscience at Oxford University in the UK, shares this point of view. ‘Over 30% of the medical problems that doctors are faced with stem directly or indirectly from sleep. But sleep has been ignored in medical training, and there are very few centres where sleep is studied,’ Foster says. He feels this needs to change.

睡眠心理学家 Gregg Jacobs 说，如果“人必须连续长时间睡眠”这一观念让夜间醒来的人感到焦虑，它就可能造成伤害，因为焦虑本身会妨碍睡眠，也很可能影响清醒时的生活。Jacobs 认为，两段睡眠之间的清醒期让人们单纯休息、放松，可能曾对人类自然调节压力的能力发挥重要作用。英国牛津大学（Oxford University）昼夜节律（生物钟）神经科学教授 Russell Foster 赞同这一看法。“医生面对的医学问题中，超过30%直接或间接源于睡眠。然而，医学培训一直忽视睡眠，研究睡眠的中心也寥寥无几，”Foster 说。他认为，这种情况必须改变。

Historical patterns of interrupted sleep

Historical evidence seems to show that humans had common sleep patterns which were quite unlike today’s eight hours of unbroken sleep each night. People habitually went to bed early, soon after 24, slept for a while, then woke up for a few hours of activity or perhaps to consider, for example, the meaning of their 25. There was little else to do at home in the dark, since what little means of lighting they had, such as using candles, was costly. In 16th-century Europe, people tended to stay at home at night because they feared 26 on largely unlit city streets. Once urban street lighting improved, nightlife in cities became popular, and recreational spots like coffee houses came to be considered fashionable. Consequently, sleeping patterns also began to change.
